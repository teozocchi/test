# 2. The Skeptic

Council session 2026-09-28 · [Idea brief](idea.md) · [Believer's case](1-believer.md)

## The number is already owned, for €5.25

The Believer's customer buys by Stiftung Warentest. This is what Warentest's 2025 face test offered her:

| Product | Who verified the SPF | Price, 50 ml |
|---|---|---|
| dm Sundance Med Ultra Sensitiv fluid, LSF 50+ | Warentest: "gut", named the price winner | €5.25 |
| La Roche-Posay Anthelios UVMune 400 fluid, LSF 50+ | Warentest: among the good results | €15–19 online |
| Eucerin Oil Control gel-cream, LSF 50+ | Warentest: a test winner | €22.45 |
| Beauty of Joseon Relief Sun | Two labs it paid, results published; stocked at dm, Rossmann, Müller and Douglas | €10.82–19.99 |
| **First Principle** | **Labs it chooses and pays; no referee has tested it** | **Not set; the Believer benchmarks €20–36** |

Müller's store-brand Lavozon fluid and Garnier's Ambre Solaire fluid were also test winners. Warentest says Lavozon has since been reformulated.

The candidate screen lists this pattern as a reason to reject: "The honest product already exists, cheaply: fluoride toothpaste, foam earplugs, Corsi-Rosenthal boxes, Stiftung Warentest's €100 purifiers." Sunscreen is Stiftung Warentest's €5 sunscreen. The purifier died because a €100 Warentest winner and a £251 box already owned its number. This idea has the same shape.

Once the referee counts, two of the three re-entry checks fail:
- **Check 2** asks for landed cost at no more than half "the net price the market already pays".
  - For a referee-verified SPF 50+ face fluid, the market pays €5.25, or €4.41 net of German VAT. Half of that is €2.20, against the brief's €2–4 unit cost before any testing.
  - The check only passes at €20 or more. At that price buyers pay for texture and brand (Eucerin, La Roche-Posay, BoJ), and First Principle has neither.
- **Check 3** asks that "no measured competitor already delivers it for less". Sundance Med does, and the referee this customer trusts measured it. The screen passed check 3 "narrowly" because it only counted brands that publish their own lab data. It left out the referee.

## Who won't pay

- **The Believer's own customer, the Warentest reader.** She trusts Warentest because it buys off the shelf and nobody pays it for the verdict. First Principle offers the reverse: samples it chooses, sent to labs it pays.
  - That is how Lean Screen was certified. Princeton Consumer Research, a UK lab, had certified at least eight of the sunscreens that failed CHOICE's test, and the TGA said it had "significant concerns" about its testing. Three paid labs are still paid labs.
  - She won't get a referee verdict on First Principle for years. Warentest picks products mainly by how widely they're sold.
  - The failures she worries about are already easy to avoid:
    - Warentest's two failed face creams were both zinc-only formulas.
    - In Tænk's marketplace test, four of the seven products tested for SPF measured 1.2–1.6. All seven were bought on Temu, Shein or AliExpress.
  - The Believer's i+m example is First Principle's future. When the referee fails a brand, the brand answers with its own ISO 24444 report, and the headline still goes to the referee.
- **The Danish buyer.** Tænk tests 60 sunscreens a year, has used HDRS since 2024, and also grades chemistry. All three face sunscreens it failed in June 2026 contained a filter suspected of disrupting hormones.
  - Matas, Denmark's leading beauty chain, features Tænk's "Bedst i test" picks and has its own Asthma-Allergy Denmark section.
  - Derma, a repeat Tænk winner, carries the Nordic Swan label on every sun product. The Swan bans titanium dioxide in sunscreens and sets biodegradability requirements for UV filters.
  - On that shelf, choosing filters "purely by measurement" (the Believer's phrase) counts against a brand.
- **The K-beauty enthusiast.** Euromonitor ranked Beauty of Joseon the number-one K-beauty suncare brand in online sales for 2025. It got there after the Purito scandal, and the coverage credits its light texture, glowy finish and price. After the biggest trust shock in her category, she simply moved to the next pleasant tube with a lab PDF, at €11–20.
- **The science-literate buyer.** She knows the amount she applies matters more than the lab number.
  - People apply 0.39–1.0 mg/cm², between a fifth and a half of the 2 mg/cm² test dose.
  - Sunscreens labelled SPF 70 and SPF 100 averaged SPF 19 and 27 when applied at 0.5 mg/cm².
  - The Believer's own arithmetic (SPF 50 lets through 2% of burning UV, SPF 60 lets through 1.7%) shows her that the gap between two honest labels is small next to how much she puts on.
  - She'll pay for a texture that makes her apply enough. She won't pay for a third lab.
- **Almost everyone, for part of the year.** In Copenhagen the UV index peaks at 0–2 from November to February. Berlin only gets moderate UV (3 or more) between March and October. The WHO recommends sun protection from 3 upwards.
  - House rule 6 says: "If the honest answer is 'you don't need this,' we say it."
  - In the launch countries, the screen's claim that daily facial SPF "avoids most of the summer seasonality" doesn't survive that rule.
  - Some scientists argue that UV below 3 still does damage. Selling on that argument would be the contested-biology marketing the brand exists to fight.

## What already solves it

| The pitch | Already exists |
|---|---|
| An SPF verified by someone other than the brand | Consumer testers buy off the shelf every summer, and the results reach the press free: Warentest (Germany), Tænk (Denmark), Which? (UK) and Euroconsumers' four member groups (Belgium, Italy, Spain, Portugal). Which? retested both of its 2025 failures before naming them. |
| A cheap tube that passed the referee | dm Sundance Med at €5.25. Lidl and dm store brands were among the winners of Warentest's body-sunscreen test at under €2 per 100 ml. |
| Several labs per product | Naked Sundays advertises "up to 5 independent tests at accredited labs" for all its sunscreens. Ultra Violette now tests every new product twice, at independent BIPEA-audited labs. Purito publishes results from KIDCS (Korea) and ABICH (Italy), and BoJ publishes two labs. A 2016 study already recommended combining results from 3–4 labs. |
| Conservative labelling | US rules already make every brand label below the mean minus a one-sided 95% margin. dm lists BoJ as "LSF 50". |
| Periodic testing and lab oversight | Australia's TGA has proposed both; its consultation closed on 23 May 2026. The Commission is reportedly revising its 2006 Recommendation, and Euroconsumers has met the Commission about "the sunscreen scandal". If the EU follows Australia, "verified" becomes the minimum for every brand. |
| A number that doesn't depend on the lab | ISO 23675, the Double Plate method, is faster and cheaper than in vivo testing. Cosmetics Europe tells its members to phase it in as a preferred alternative. |

## The rule kills its own product

The working line is "Our label is lower than our worst test". The plan tests every batch at several labs and publishes every result. Here is what the measured spread between labs does to that promise.

**The spread:**
- **In vivo testing is noisy.** The ALT-SPF ring study measured the in vivo test's reproducibility at 0.27 on a natural-log scale, and 0.18–0.44 depending on product group (Cole 2025).
- **Single products can swing wildly.** In a seven-lab study, one SPF 50+ product measured anywhere from 5.5 to 62.4 (Trullàs 2020).
- **Proficiency testing agrees.** In BIPEA's proficiency scheme the spread between labs has stayed at 10–50% since 2009.
- **So the founder may be right about variability.** If so, the rule will sometimes produce a disastrous reading on a good product.

**What that spread does to "worst of three"** (my calculation, assuming lognormal results, independent labs and a spread of 0.27; "true SPF" means the formula's average across labs):

| True SPF | One batch: all 3 labs read 50 or more | 3 years, 1 batch a year (9 results): at least one below 50 | 3 years, 4 batches a year (36 results): at least one below 50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 42% | 92% | ~100% |
| 70 | 71% | 64% | 98% |
| 80 | 88% | 31% | 78% |
| 100 | 98% | 5% | 17% |

Real life is harsher than this model:
- The Believer's extra HDRS test lowers every pass rate.
- A lab that consistently reads low makes things worse again.
- The extremes actually seen (Lean Screen's 4–64, the 5.5–62.4 above) are wider than a lognormal model predicts.
- For the most variable product group (spread 0.44), even a true SPF 100 passes a single batch only about 84% of the time.

To earn "50+" honestly in the EU, a formula needs a measured 60 or more. BoJ's two labs read 52.5 and 63.1. That leaves the rule with three ways out, all bad:
1. **Make the formula about twice as strong as the label.** Printing "50" safely needs a true SPF near 100. That means more filter in a daily fluid, which fights the texture facial SPF sells on. Nobody has quoted a unit cost for it (estimate; no formulator has been asked).
2. **Print "30".** The Believer's case is built to avoid "our 30" against "their 50+".
3. **Print "50" on an ordinary formula with a true SPF of 60–70.** Within a year or two the brand's own page would show, for example, its own lab reading SPF 38 under a "50" label. The purifier's gotcha was "listed 35, measured 39". This one is worse, because the brand's own lab produces it.

A blocked batch costs the launch. Under the Believer's rule, "a batch that tests below its band doesn't ship". A 25,000-unit batch at €2–4 a unit is €50–100k, most of the brief's €65–165k launch cash. The way out is to retest until something passes. Ultra Violette did exactly that with Lean Screen, eight tests ranging from SPF 4 to 64, and it's the practice this brand exists to attack.

## Noisy method or reproducible method, the edge disappears

- **If the method is noisy, the rule above bites.** That covers in vivo testing and HDRS, the method Warentest and Tænk use.
  - HDRS was validated as no worse than in vivo testing, not as better.
  - In the ALT-SPF blinded study of the LED version, labs differed by 20% of the mean on average after improvements (39% before). All but one lab tended to read low.
  - So a single referee result can fail a genuinely good tube. An ordinary brand answers one bad grade with a statement, as i+m and Junglück did. A brand whose only product is the number can't.
  - The Believer's own line, "brands that did well in earlier years go on to fail", is what that noise looks like from outside.
- **If the method is reproducible, the edge is gone.** The Double Plate method (ISO 23675) showed "very high intra- and inter-laboratory reproducibility". One test at any lab then gives any brand the same confidence as First Principle's three.
  - "Several labs" becomes a ritual for a problem the industry is retiring.
  - The ritual has an ethical cost. The EU Recommendation itself says "preference should be given to in vitro testing methods delivering equivalent results, as in vivo methods raise ethical concerns". The HDRS validation paper notes that the UV used in in vivo tests raises the volunteers' skin-cancer risk.
  - The launch plan still exposes two or three volunteer panels, of at least 10 people each, to that UV.

Either way, the "why now" belongs to the incumbents:
- HDRS came out of the Dr. Schrader institute and Courage + Khazaka, with Beiersdorf (Nivea, and Eucerin, a 2025 test winner) among the collaborators.
- The ALT-SPF consortium that validated both new methods includes BASF and Beiersdorf.

## What the founder is too close to see

1. **"The claims are physics" isn't true of the headline test.** In vivo SPF is a biological test. A technician judges by eye how red volunteers' backs turn; the brief says so itself. Only ISO 23675 is purely optical, so the brand's physics-not-biology frame mislabels its own evidence.
2. **The number isn't the protection.** In April 2026 Brian Diffey (Newcastle University) proposed replacing the SPF number with a binary label. The search summary of his paper calls the labelled SPF "a meaningless number" for real-world protection, where people apply "varying amounts of sunscreen non-uniformly". A leading photobiologist wants that number off the label, and the brand is "sold on one verified number".
3. **The line that would sell the product is the hardest one to say legally.** This is my reading, not legal advice.
   - Regulation 655/2013 says: "Claims which convey the idea that a product has a specific benefit when this benefit is mere compliance with minimum legal requirements shall not be allowed." Its fairness criterion also bars denigrating competitors.
   - In Germany, advertising a feature the law already requires (*Werbung mit Selbstverständlichkeiten*) is recognised as misleading under the UWG, the unfair-competition law, and invites warning letters.
   - The Believer's line, "a 50 that passed every test" against "a 50+ that passed one", implies that every competitor's label is unreliable.
   - "Our label is lower than our worst test" can probably be said. The comparison that would sell it probably can't.
4. **The record doesn't compound.** Every reformulation and every change of method resets it.
   - The filter list keeps moving. 4-MBC has been banned since May 2025, homosalate has been limited to face products at a lower concentration, and octocrylene faces a proposed ECHA restriction.
   - The industry is moving from ISO 24444 to ISO 23675.
   - "Three years of batches" would be three years of an outgoing method, on a formula that may have to change.
5. **The release test measures the tube before it ships, not the tube in the customer's bag.** Bondi Sands recalled five batches in November 2025 because the lotion separated, which the TGA said could affect the SPF. Batch-release data would have shown a pass. The referee buys from a shop shelf months later, and the customer uses the tube after a summer in a hot car.
6. **The brand was built for a different category.**
   - The thesis is "Industrial-grade hardware for human biology", and the style rules ban "glowing people" and glowing-skin photos. Yet facial SPF's bestsellers sell glow, like BoJ's glowy finish and Ultra Violette's "Luminising" Queen Screen.
   - The founder's strength, measurement, would be handed to outside labs.
   - What actually sells, texture, would come from a contract manufacturer's base formula. That is where the Lean Screen and Purito failures started.
   - First Principle would own a disclosure policy and a folder of PDFs.
7. **Nothing exists yet.**
   - Budget, team, formulator, manufacturer and timeline are all "not yet stated".
   - Custom sunscreen development takes 12–18 months, including at least six months of accelerated stability testing (vendor sources).
   - Summer 2027 is tight; 2028 is the realistic launch (estimate).

## The recurring revenue is smaller than it looks

- **Tube life.** The Believer's six to eight weeks assumes the 2 mg/cm² test dose.
  - At the 0.4–1.0 mg/cm² people actually apply, a 50 ml tube lasts about three to seven months (my calculation, assuming about 600 cm² of skin).
  - Leave out the low-UV months, and a customer buys one to three tubes a year (estimate).
- **Testing cost per tube.** "1–3% of the price" only holds for 25,000-unit batches.
  - One German contract manufacturer advertises runs from 30 kg, about 600 tubes, and minimum orders of 1,000–10,000 units are common.
  - For a 5,000-unit batch, three in vivo labs at the brief's $2,700–4,500 each add $1.60–2.70 per tube. That's close to the €2–4 it costs to make one.
  - At one testing vendor's $5,000–15,000 per in vivo study, it's $3–9 per tube (estimates).
- **Selling a 25,000-unit batch.** At two or three tubes a year, that takes 8,000–12,500 active customers (estimate).
  - Skincare direct-to-consumer brands pay about $35–70 to acquire a customer (industry benchmark blogs).
  - That's roughly €250–750k of acquisition spending, against €65–165k of launch cash (estimate).
  - The project has no audience; the purifier never launched.

## Where the Believer's case breaks

- **"Those proxies fail at every price."** The Warentest proxy didn't fail; it caught Junglück and i+m. Buying what Warentest rated "gut" worked, and one of those tubes cost €5.25.
- **"Beauty of Joseon… labelling below its worst result would make it print 50."** dm already lists it as "LSF 50", at €18.95. That's the listing title; I couldn't see the pack, and Rossmann lists it as 50+. Either way, it isn't a barrier.
- **"Disclosure is the one thing incumbents can't copy cheaply."** The Believer's own figure, 1–3% of the price, says copying is cheap. Naked Sundays, Ultra Violette, Purito and BoJ already do parts of it.
- **"The optical method takes the technician's eye out of the reading."** It doesn't take the lab out of the reading: HDRS results vary between labs about as much as in vivo results do.
- **"Retesting every batch no longer means sunburning a fresh panel."** That's only true if the batch test is HDRS or in vitro. The brief promises "every batch at several labs", and the launch plan still uses two or three in vivo panels.
- **"Good sunscreen physics is already for sale: 8 of the 14 face creams… were rated 'gut'."** That sentence is the case against the idea.
- **Natural brands "can't switch to the filters that measure best".** They aren't competing for a buyer who reads Warentest, because Warentest already failed them in public.
- **"The brand journalists call after the next failed test table."** Journalists call the referee and the brands that failed. A brand the referee hasn't tested isn't in that story.

## The fastest way this dies

It dies at the first lab round, before a single tube is sold.
- The brand's own rule sends the first formula to two or three in vivo labs, plus an HDRS test.
- An honest EU "50+" has a true SPF of about 60–70. At that level, the chance that all three in vivo labs read 50 or more is 42–71%, and lower once HDRS is added (my calculation).
- One low reading either puts "30" on the box, or sends the formula back for another development round and six more months of stability testing.
- The purifier died on a fan selector. This one dies on a lab report the brand commissioned and, under its own rule, has to publish.

If the formula survives that round, it dies later in the same way. The published record keeps growing, and its worst result can only go down.

Outsiders can speed this up. In 2020 the founder of INCIDecoder paid two labs and ended Purito's SPF 50+ claim. Anyone can do the same to First Principle, and the brand's own batch page will tell them which batch to buy.

## The fatal flaw

**The verified SPF is already owned: by referees nobody pays, who buy off the shelf, and by the €5.25 tube that passes them.**
- Warentest rated eight face sunscreens "gut" in 2025, one of them at €5.25. Tænk tests 60 sunscreens a year, and Which? and Euroconsumers cover much of the rest of Western Europe.
- The skincare-literate buyer trusts those verdicts because the brand didn't pay for them.
- All First Principle can add is more tests that it pays for. That is weaker evidence than a single referee result.
- At the measured spread between labs, those tests are also bound to put a result below the brand's own label, on its own page, unless the formula is about twice as strong as the label.

Every piece of evidence here says this buyer trusts the referee over a brand's lab. If so, "more tests" earns no premium and check 3 fails. It's the purifier again: the honest product already exists, cheaply. Don't build it.
